EDPB work programme 2026-2027 (consent or pay guidelines in preparation)

12 February 2026EURegulator guidanceconsent or payconsent

The work programme adopted on 12 February 2026 lists guidelines on consent or pay among the guidance the Board will deliver in the 2026-2027 cycle, extending the reasoning of Opinion 08/2024 beyond large online platforms. It binds no one and contains no rule, but it tells publishers running paid ad-free tiers that a general EDPB position covering their model is coming. Keep any consent or pay implementation aligned with Opinion 08/2024 now, including a free alternative with less intrusive advertising where feasible, and plan for a review when the guidelines are published for consultation.

EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 on the Digital Omnibus proposal

10 February 2026EURegulator guidancebrowser signalsanalytics exemptionconsent

The joint opinion supports moving terminal equipment rules into the GDPR and a regulatory answer to consent fatigue, welcoming the six-month bar on repeat requests after a refusal in proposed Article 88a(4) and strongly supporting machine-readable preference signals in Article 88b. It asks that the Article 88a(3) exemptions be narrow: audience measurement limited to aggregated data not combined across services, and any contextual advertising exemption clearly delimited. It objects strongly to the proposed narrowing of the definition of personal data and to delegating that definition to Commission implementing acts. It binds no one, but it signals how supervisory authorities will read the amended rules if adopted.

EDPB Recommendations 2/2025 on the legal basis for requiring the creation of user accounts on e-commerce websites

3 December 2025EURegulator guidanceconsentdark patternsother

Adopted on 3 December 2025 for a public consultation that closed on 12 February 2026, the recommendations find that a mandatory account for a one-off purchase is unlikely to pass the necessity test under Article 6(1)(b) (paragraph 23), that Article 6(1)(c) applies only exceptionally (paragraph 47), and that order tracking, loyalty, future purchases and fraud prevention do not meet the Article 6(1)(f) necessity and balancing tests (paragraphs 58 to 75). Digital Omnibus proposal, COM(2025) 837, 2025/0360(COD): new GDPR Articles 88a and 88b on terminal equipment and machine-readable consent signals

19 November 2025EUProposalproposedbrowser signalsanalytics exemptionconsent

The proposal moves the rules on storing and accessing personal data on terminal equipment from Article 5(3) ePrivacy into a new GDPR Article 88a, which keeps consent as the default, exempts transmission, requested services, aggregated first-party audience measurement and security, and bars a fresh consent request for the same purpose for at least six months after a refusal. A new Article 88b obliges controllers to honour automated, machine-readable consent and refusal signals once standards exist, with a six-month grace period, and requires browser providers other than SMEs to offer the feature. As of September 2026 the file remains in committee with no Council general approach and no trilogue.

EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on the technical scope of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, version 2.0

7 October 2024EURegulator guidancepixels wiretapstrictly necessaryconsent

The guidelines set four criteria for Article 5(3): information, whether personal or not; terminal equipment of a user; a connection to a public communications network; and storage of, or gaining access to, that information. Paragraphs 47 to 63 apply them to URL and pixel tracking, local processing whose output is sent to a third party, tracking based on IP address alone, IoT reporting and unique identifiers, and find that each falls within Article 5(3) where the instruction to send information comes from code the entity distributed. EDPB Opinion 08/2024 on valid consent in the context of consent or pay models implemented by large online platforms

17 April 2024EURegulator guidanceconsent or payconsentcookie walls

Requested by the Dutch, Norwegian and Hamburg authorities, the opinion finds that in most cases a large online platform offering only a choice between behavioural advertising and a fee will not obtain freely given consent, and that platforms should consider an equivalent alternative without a fee, in particular a free version with advertising that processes less or no personal data (paragraphs 73 to 77). Any fee must not inhibit free choice (paragraph 85), and imbalance of power, lock-in and network effects weigh against validity (paragraphs 99 to 112). Authorities apply its reasoning to publishers. Model any paid alternative against the free, less intrusive option; general EDPB guidelines are in preparation.

The Court held that the TC String generated under the Transparency and Consent Framework is personal data within Article 4(1) GDPR, because combined with an identifier such as an IP address it allows the user to be identified, and that IAB Europe is a joint controller with its members for the recording of users' consent preferences in the TC String, though not for the later processing by participants unless it influences its purposes and means. CJEU, Case C-252/21, Meta Platforms and Others v Bundeskartellamt

The Grand Chamber held that a competition authority may examine GDPR compliance when assessing abuse of dominance, that personalised advertising which finances a social network is not necessary for the contract under Article 6(1)(b) and cannot be justified as a legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) for the data at issue, and that a dominant position does not by itself invalidate consent but is an important factor in judging whether it was freely given, with the burden on the operator. Digital Markets Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/1925, Article 5(2) (consent for combining and cross-using personal data)

2 May 2023EULegislationconsentconsent or payenforcement

Article 5(2) prohibits a gatekeeper from processing third-party data for advertising through its core platform services, combining personal data across its services or with third-party data, cross-using data between services, or signing users in to other services to combine data, unless the user has been presented with a specific choice and has given GDPR-standard consent. The guidelines name six families of deceptive design pattern, overloading, skipping, stirring, obstructing, fickle and left in the dark, and assess each against Article 5(1)(a) fairness and transparency, Article 12 transparency, Article 25 data protection by design and default, and the Articles 4(11) and 7 consent conditions. 17 January 2023EURegulator guidancereject buttondark patternsstrictly necessary

The report records the positions supervisory authorities share on the noyb banner complaints: a first layer with an accept button but no reject option is non-compliant (paragraphs 6 to 8); pre-ticked boxes do not create consent (paragraphs 9 to 10); a reject option hidden as a small link or outside the banner is insufficient (paragraphs 11 to 14); unreadable button contrast is manifestly misleading (paragraph 18); legitimate interest cannot ground a cookie under Article 5(3) (paragraphs 20 to 25); operators must prove and keep updated the list of essential cookies (paragraph 27); and withdrawal must be as easy as consent (paragraph 34). EDPB Guidelines 8/2020 on the targeting of social media users, version 2.0

13 April 2021EURegulator guidancepixels wiretapconsentother

The guidelines apply Wirtschaftsakademie and Fashion ID to advertising: a targeter that embeds a social media pixel or plug-in jointly determines the purpose of the collection and transmission with the platform and is a joint controller for those operations, though not for what the platform does afterwards. CJEU, Case C-61/19, Orange Romania SA v ANSPDCP

11 November 2020EUCourt decisionconsentdark patterns

The Court held under Articles 4(11) and 6(1)(a) GDPR (and the equivalent Directive 95/46 provisions) that the controller must prove the data subject consented by active behaviour after receiving information in clear and plain language, and that a contract clause recording consent does not prove it where the controller pre-ticked the box, where the terms could mislead the customer about being able to contract without consenting, or where refusing required completing an extra form. The case concerned identity documents in a telecoms contract, but the reasoning applies to any consent flow. Keep the refusal path no longer than the acceptance path and retain evidence of the affirmative act.

EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent under Regulation 2016/679

4 May 2020EURegulator guidanceconsentcookie wallsreject button

The guidelines spell out the Article 4(11) and Article 7 conditions and add two positions that shape banners directly: access to a website cannot be conditioned on accepting cookies (a cookie wall does not produce freely given consent, paragraph 39), and scrolling or swiping through a page is not a clear affirmative action (paragraph 86). They also require granular purposes, a documented record, and withdrawal as easy as giving consent. They bind all controllers relying on consent, including for the GDPR processing that follows a cookie. Use them as the checklist for banner wording, button design and consent logs. They replace Article 29 Working Party Guidelines WP259 on consent.

CJEU, Case C-673/17, Bundesverband der Verbraucherzentralen v Planet49 GmbH

1 October 2019EUCourt decisionconsentreject button

The Court held that consent under Article 5(3) ePrivacy, read with Article 2(h) of Directive 95/46 and Article 4(11) GDPR, is not validly given by a pre-ticked checkbox the user must deselect, and that this applies whether or not the information stored or accessed is personal data. It also held that the information the operator must give includes the duration of the cookies and whether third parties may have access to them. The ruling binds every operator setting cookies on EU users' devices and is the source of the active opt-in rule. Check that no toggle is preset to on and that the banner discloses lifetimes and third-party recipients.

CJEU, Case C-40/17, Fashion ID GmbH & Co. KG v Verbraucherzentrale NRW eV

29 July 2019EUCourt decisionpixels wiretapconsentother

The Court held that a website operator embedding a third-party social plug-in that causes the visitor's browser to send personal data to the plug-in provider is a joint controller with that provider for the collection and disclosure by transmission, but not for the provider's subsequent processing. The operator must itself provide the information due at collection and, where consent is the basis, obtain it for the collection and transmission it controls. It binds every site that embeds a third-party pixel, plug-in or script that fires before consent. Gate every third-party tag behind consent, name the recipient in the notice, and agree the Article 26 allocation with the vendor.

EDPB Opinion 5/2019 on the interplay between the ePrivacy Directive and the GDPR

12 March 2019EURegulator guidanceconsentenforcementother

The opinion holds that where the ePrivacy Directive particularises the GDPR the specific rule prevails, so where Article 5(3) requires consent for storing or accessing information on a device, no other Article 6 GDPR basis can replace it, and the later processing of the personal data collected still needs its own GDPR basis. It also confirms that the GDPR one-stop-shop and consistency mechanism do not extend to ePrivacy enforcement, which stays with the national authority each Member State designates. Binds supervisory authorities and, through them, every operator. Expect parallel national enforcement of cookie rules without a lead authority, and never justify a tracking cookie on legitimate interest.

General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 4(11), 6, 7 and 25 and Recital 30

25 May 2018EULegislationconsentdark patternsother

Article 4(11) defines consent as freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, shown by a statement or clear affirmative action, and Article 7 adds the burden of proof, separability, and withdrawal as easy as giving consent. Recital 30 names cookie identifiers and IP addresses as identifiers that may make a person identifiable, so tracking data is personal data and Article 6 requires a legal basis for what follows collection. Article 25 requires privacy by design and by default, applied by regulators to default banner settings. Applies from 25 May 2018 to every controller processing EU residents' data; fines reach 20 million euro or 4 per cent of turnover.

CJEU, Case C-582/14, Patrick Breyer v Bundesrepublik Deutschland (dynamic IP addresses)

19 October 2016EUCourt decisionotheranalytics exemption

The Court held that a dynamic IP address recorded by a website operator is personal data in relation to that operator where it has legal means to obtain from the access provider the additional information needed to identify the visitor, and that national law cannot limit the legitimate interest ground so as to exclude retention of that data to keep the site working and defend it against attacks. It binds every operator logging visitor IP addresses, and Recital 30 GDPR and EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 build on it. Treat IP-based analytics, fraud checks and server logs as personal data processing with a stated legal basis and retention period.

ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC as amended by Directive 2009/136/EC, Article 5(3) and Article 13

19 December 2009EULegislationconsentstrictly necessaryemail marketing

Article 5(3) permits the storing of information, or the gaining of access to information already stored, in a user's terminal equipment only with consent given after clear and comprehensive information, with exceptions for transmission of a communication and for storage or access strictly necessary for a service explicitly requested by the user. Article 13(1) requires prior consent for automated calls, fax and email marketing, and Article 13(2) sets the soft opt-in for email marketing of own similar products to existing customers. The rules bind anyone placing cookies, pixels or scripts on EU users' devices, whatever the sender's location. Map every tag against the two exemptions and keep the consent record.
